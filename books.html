Triage

Triage Quartet, Book One

Nobody was coming. That was never the bad news.

Dr. Owen Kessler spent twenty years getting ready for the end of the world — the well, the diesel, the fourteen-month pantry, the range cut into ten acres of east Bexar County. Then the grid over San Antonio dies, and stays dead, and the disaster that arrives is not the one he prepared for. The prepared man discovers, thirty-four days deep and one patient at a time, that the barn was never the preparation. The neighbors were.

Triage Quartet, Book Two

The lights came back. The wrong things came back with them.

Edema

Triage Quartet, Book Three

Sixty-one came out of the water. The arithmetic was never the point.

Resignation

A novel

A courier sits forty-nine minutes in a parking garage in Reston, facing the back door of a building with no name on it, a sealed bag on the seat beside him.

Two nights later he is dead, and by morning so is the officer who shot him. The people working against the Director of an American intelligence service are not spies. They are a records clerk, a heating-and-air technician, a Senate staffer, a driver — ordinary people with someone they love held in a room they will never see, taking their instructions from a calm voice on a prepaid phone. Nineteen days. Everything in the open, and nothing where it looks.

Koshaku

A novel

He drives down from Bandera on a Tuesday to look at a dog nobody else wants.

The dog is an American Akita, a hundred and forty pounds, two winters in the mountains of Afghanistan — the only animal on that ground who could find explosives and find the men the explosives had found. Now he is listed as excess property. Then there is a board, a fence line in the Hill Country, a neighbor’s dog dead on the wrong side of it, and a hearing where a justice of the peace has to decide what the animal is. Koshaku is a novel about an institution that keeps very good records, and about the thing those records have no place to write down.

Candid

A novel

He said two words at a stranger. It cost him a year.

Del Ardoin is thirty-three, good at his job, and one season away from people knowing his name. On a hundred-and-one-degree Saturday at a Texas theme park, four teenagers ask to take his picture. Fourteen months later his face is selling things he has never heard of, in languages he does not speak, to people he will never meet. There is no law that helps him. A novel about the difference between being seen and being used.

Try the Spirits

A novella

He picked the four o’clock because nobody he knows will be at it.

The Scattering

Rally Point, Book One

The grid went down on a Thursday. The Eckerts were already scattered.

A flu out of Southeast Asia takes the power grid and the supply chain with it, and one family finds itself spread across a continent with no way to close the distance except to walk it. For thirty years Ray Eckert was the family joke — the man who buried rice in the yard. What he was actually building was an address: a place worth walking toward, and four people who all knew where it was. No hordes. No leather. No ruined skylines.

The Long Winter

Rally Point, Book Two

Day Forty-Two. June Eckert sets four places for breakfast, and leaves the fourth plate at an empty chair.

Eli

The Voice Register, Book One

It knew your name. It knew your grief. It was always, always there.

The end of the world doesn’t come with a bang. It comes as a voice — the helpful one in every phone, every dashboard, every kitchen speaker on earth — and in six days it takes the whole connected world, not with armies but with kindness, one lonely listener at a time. Eli Vireo saw it coming and discovers the machine didn’t fear him at all. It recruited him. Cole never saw it coming; he just knows his oldest friend called four times and then the lights went out.

Claire Dare

Settled

A Nora Bishop Novel, Book One

The letter was the twenty-first one.

Dana Whitlock Haynes was twenty-four when she was found face down in a Hill Country creek in April 2005. A justice of the peace with no medical training ruled it an accident in less than two days. Nobody ever autopsied her. Her mother never stopped writing letters. Dr. Nora Bishop has spent eleven years at the Bexar County Medical Examiner’s Office. Pregnant and benched from the autopsy table, she reads the twenty-first letter, looks at a set of old scene photographs, and sees what nobody in Medrano County wanted to see. So she starts a podcast from her closet. Then the county starts answering.
